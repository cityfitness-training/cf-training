The Reframe

Not a Failure.
A Strategy.

The moment you frame the T.O. as something that happens when you failed, you have already lost its power. The T.O. is a deliberate, high-leverage play that brings a fresh dynamic into a stalled situation. It is the third ask — and it works when nothing else can because it changes everything: the voice, the authority, and the angle.

The Standard
A T.O. is initiated after the consultant has given a full tour, presented price, asked for the sale, applied LIGO twice, and asked for the sale a third time — and the guest is still hesitant. At that point, the T.O. is not optional. It is the play.

01 What Is the T.O. and Why Does It Work? Foundation ▼

A Turn Over (T.O.) is the strategic introduction of a manager into a stalled sales interaction. It is invoked after the consultant has fully executed the sales process — tour, B+F, HACCL, price, LIGO, re-close — and the guest remains hesitant. The T.O. gives the sale a final, powerful attempt through a new dynamic that the consultant alone cannot create.

01
Fresh Voice
A new voice breaks the pattern. The guest has been in conversation with one person for 45+ minutes. A fresh perspective can shift the dynamic and re-engage someone who has mentally checked out of the existing conversation.
02
Authority Bridge
The guest perceives the manager as having higher authority — which means their words carry more weight. A concern that felt unresolvable with the consultant often dissolves when a manager addresses it directly.
03
Tailored Options
The manager has authority to offer tailored promotions, addendums, or a final compelling offer that a consultant may not have access to. This is often the difference between a stalled sale and a closed one.
The Mindset Shift
Consultants who view the T.O. as a personal failure avoid it — and lose sales that were winnable. Consultants who view it as the highest-leverage play available execute it confidently — and close more. Your ego has no place in the T.O. decision. The guest's membership does.
02 Introduce the Manager Early — Before You Need Them The Setup ▼

This is the most underutilized T.O. principle — and the one that makes the biggest difference in how the T.O. lands. The ideal time to introduce the manager is not when the sale is stalled. It is during the greeting or the pre-tour, when receptivity is at its highest and the interaction is still warm.

When the manager is introduced early — casually, naturally, as part of the welcoming process — they have already built their own small amount of rapport with the guest before any sales pressure exists. That rapport becomes the foundation for an effective T.O. later if one is needed.

Early Manager Introduction — During Greeting
[Manager walks by naturally during the greeting or pre-tour.] Consultant: "Hey [Manager's Name] — I want you to meet [Guest's Name]. They're in today checking us out." Manager: "Great to meet you, [Guest's Name]! [Name] is going to take great care of you — let me know if you need anything." [Manager moves on naturally. No pressure. Just a warm, human moment.]
The Ambush Problem
Introducing the manager for the first time when the price sheet is on the table and the guest has just said no is an ambush. The guest feels cornered. Two people are now applying pressure where one was failing. This triggers a flight response — not a close. The early introduction prevents this entirely.
The Goal of the Early Introduction
When the T.O. happens later, the guest should feel like they are talking to someone they already know slightly — not a stranger being deployed to close them. That familiarity is the difference between a warm T.O. and a cold one. Warm T.O.s close. Cold T.O.s create resistance.
03 When to Call the T.O. — Exact Timing Timing ▼

The T.O. has a specific trigger point. It is not a general "call the manager in when things get hard." It is a precise moment in the sales sequence that, when reached, activates the play.

The T.O. Trigger Point
The consultant has: (1) delivered a full tour, (2) executed B+F and HACCL, (3) presented the price, (4) asked for the sale, (5) applied LIGO, (6) asked for the sale a second time, (7) applied LIGO a second time, (8) asked for the sale a third time — and the guest is still hesitant. That is the moment. Not before.

Calling the T.O. too early — before LIGO has been genuinely applied — robs the consultant of the opportunity to close it themselves. Calling it too late — after the guest has already emotionally disengaged or committed to leaving — makes the T.O. feel desperate rather than strategic.

Reading the Room
Sometimes a guest's energy signals that a T.O. is needed before the third ask. If the guest has become visibly disengaged, is physically preparing to leave, or has said "I really do need to go" twice — do not wait for the third cycle. Invoke the T.O. now. The trigger point is a guideline, not a rigid rule. Trust your read of the room.
The Crutch Problem
Some consultants use the T.O. as a crutch — calling the manager in after the first objection to avoid having to handle it themselves. This is not a T.O. strategy. It is avoidance. The manager should be a final resource, not a substitute for learning LIGO. Reserve the T.O. for when it is genuinely needed.
04 How to Frame the T.O. — Always a Benefit, Never a Tactic The Setup ▼

How you introduce the T.O. to the guest determines whether they receive it as a resource or a pressure tactic. The framing is everything. The reason you give them for involving the manager must always feel like it is in their best interest — because it genuinely is.

There are two common T.O. situations, each with a different framing:

T.O. Script 01 — Experience/Commitment Concern
SCENARIOGuest is hesitant and mentioned wanting to try it first before fully committing.
Consultant"My ultimate goal is for you to leave today feeling genuinely excited and fully committed to coming back. So here's what I'd love to do — let me set up a complimentary access to one of our studio classes or a Thrive workout session so you can actually experience it firsthand. Does that sound like something that would help?"
Guest"Yeah, that sounds good actually."
Consultant"Perfect. You met [Manager's Name] earlier — let me grab them for a second, they're the best person to get that set up for you right now."
T.O. Script 02 — Price Concern
SCENARIOGuest has a lingering concern about the price even after B+F math was presented.
Consultant"I hear you — and I want to make sure we can make this work. Pricing is something I want to make sure you feel completely right about. I'd love to run this by [Manager's Name] real quick — they may be able to look at some options that help make this the right fit for you today. Give me just one second."
The Golden Framing Rule
The T.O. must never sound like "I couldn't close you so I'm bringing someone who can." It must always sound like "I'm going the extra mile to find a solution that works specifically for you." The guest should feel taken care of — not tag-teamed.
05 The Consultant Brief — Setting the Manager Up to Win The Handoff ▼

Before the manager steps in with the guest, the consultant needs to brief them — quickly, efficiently, and with the right information. A manager who walks in cold is at a disadvantage. A manager who walks in armed with the guest's X-Factor, their objection, and the tier being discussed closes at a dramatically higher rate.

The 30-Second Brief — What to Cover
1. Guest's name.   2. Their X-Factor (the emotional why you uncovered in discovery).   3. The membership you recommended and why.   4. The specific objection that triggered the T.O.   5. What was tried (LIGO, B+F math).   That is it. 30 seconds. Clear and specific.
Example Brief to Manager
"[Guest's Name] — she really connected with Revive and the pool. X-Factor is wanting to feel like herself again after a rough year — she has two young kids. We're on Performance+. She's hesitating on price, says it's more than she planned. I walked through the B+F math and she got it but still paused. Two rounds of LIGO. She's not a no — she just needs a fresh voice."

The manager can now walk in knowing exactly who they are talking to, what moves them, and what has already been tried. They do not waste time re-covering ground the consultant already covered. They walk in with precision and close the gap.

Brief Out of Earshot
Always step away from the guest to brief the manager. Never brief within earshot — it is awkward, it reveals your strategy, and it makes the guest feel like they are being discussed rather than helped.
06 The Consultant's Role During T.O. — Total Silence The Non-Negotiable ▼

Once the T.O. has been initiated and the manager has taken over, the consultant has exactly one job: be completely, totally, absolutely silent. Not mostly silent. Not silent except when addressed. Silent.

This is the rule most consultants break — and breaking it is one of the most damaging things that can happen to a T.O. Here is why silence is non-negotiable:

🚫
Two voices create overwhelm. When both the consultant and the manager speak, the guest feels surrounded and pressured. Even well-intentioned contributions from the consultant amplify the "two against one" dynamic that triggers the flight response.
🚫
Two voices create confusion. If the consultant adds information after the manager has started their approach, it can undercut the manager's angle or introduce conflicting information. The guest does not know who to focus on or whose version to trust.
🚫
Silence empowers the manager. When the consultant is visibly quiet, it communicates to the guest that the manager is the authority. That authority is the entire reason the T.O. works. The moment the consultant speaks, it diminishes that authority signal.
✓
The one exception. If the manager asks the consultant a direct question — specifically something only the consultant can answer, like "And she said she'd use the pool about 4 times a month, right?" — the consultant answers briefly and directly, then returns to silence immediately. This is the only acceptable break from the silence rule.
The Consultant's Body Language During T.O.
Stay present but neutral. Do not cross your arms. Do not look anxious. Do not make facial expressions that telegraph your own concern about whether the T.O. is working. Relaxed, calm, confident — as if you are completely certain the manager is going to handle this. Because they are.
T.O. in Action — Complete Flow with Manager Taking Over
Consultant"[Guest's Name], I appreciate you sharing that with me. I want to make sure we can make this work — I'd love to bring [Manager's Name] over for a second. They may be able to look at some options I'm not able to offer. Give me just a moment."
ACTIONConsultant steps away. Briefs manager in 30 seconds. Returns to the table with manager. Sits back slightly. Does not speak.
Manager"[Guest's Name], it's great to see you again — [Consultant's Name] told me a little about what you're looking for. I just want to take a second to understand where you're at, because I want to make sure we find the right fit." [Manager continues. Consultant is silent throughout.]
Guest"I really love it here, I just wasn't planning on spending quite this much today."
Manager"I hear you — and I really appreciate you being honest with me. Let me ask you something: based on everything [Name] showed you today, is this the kind of place you could see yourself actually using consistently?"
Guest"Yeah, absolutely."
Manager"Then let's figure out how to make this happen. Here's what I can do for you today..." [Manager applies tailored offer or final option. Asks for the sale.]
NOTEConsultant remains silent throughout the entire manager interaction — even when the guest looks at them for a reaction. Neutral, calm expression. Let the manager work.

Common Mistakes
What Kills the T.O.

6 T.O. Mistakes
& How to Fix Them

✗ The Mistake
Treating the T.O. as an admission of failure. The consultant hesitates to call it, misses the window, and the guest leaves.
✓ The Fix
Reframe it as strategy, not defeat. The T.O. is the third ask — the highest-leverage play available. A confident consultant executes it without hesitation when the trigger point is reached.
✗ The Mistake
Introducing the manager for the first time at the price card when the sale is stuck. It feels like an ambush. The guest shuts down.
✓ The Fix
Always introduce the manager early — during the greeting or pre-tour. When the T.O. happens, the guest is talking to someone they already have a small connection with. That changes everything.
✗ The Mistake
Framing the T.O. as: "Let me get my manager — maybe they can help since I can't." You just told the guest you failed. They lose confidence in both of you.
✓ The Fix
Always frame as a benefit to the guest. "I want to see if there's anything we can do to make this the right fit for you today — let me bring [Name] in for a moment." Service framing. Not failure framing.
✗ The Mistake
Not briefing the manager before they step in. The manager walks in cold, re-covers ground already covered, and the guest feels their time is being wasted.
✓ The Fix
Always brief out of earshot — 30 seconds, five specific points: name, X-Factor, recommended tier, specific objection, what was already tried. The manager closes the gap — not re-opens the conversation.
✗ The Mistake
Speaking during the T.O. to add context or correct the manager. Two voices create overwhelm and undermine the manager's authority — the only thing that makes the T.O. work.
✓ The Fix
Total silence once the manager takes over. The one exception: if the manager asks you a direct question only you can answer. Otherwise — nothing. Not a word. Let the manager work.
✗ The Mistake
Using the T.O. as a crutch — calling the manager in after the first objection instead of handling it with LIGO. This makes you dependent and stunts your growth as a closer.
✓ The Fix
The T.O. is the final play — not the first response. Apply LIGO twice and ask for the sale three times yourself before involving the manager. The T.O. is earned by exhausting your own options first.
Role-Play Scenarios
Practice

4 T.O. Scenarios
to Train On

Scenario 01 — Price Concern T.O. Most Common
Two full rounds of LIGO on a price concern. B+F math presented twice. Third ask made. Guest says: "I genuinely appreciate everything, but I'm just not ready to do this financially today."
Your objective: Frame the T.O. as a resource for options: "I hear you — and I don't want price to be the thing that stands between you and a decision you've told me you want to make. Let me bring [Manager's Name] in — they may have some options I'm not able to offer. One second." Brief the manager with: name, X-Factor, Performance+, price hesitation, B+F math already applied twice. Then sit back and be completely silent while the manager works.
Practice the physical discipline of silence here. During the role-play, the "manager" (played by a peer) should occasionally look at you or address you indirectly. Practice maintaining a neutral, confident expression without breaking the silence or making supportive facial expressions that undercut the manager's authority.
Scenario 02 — Experience/Commitment T.O. Try-First Hesitation
Guest has been enthusiastic throughout but keeps circling back to: "I just want to try it first before I commit. Can I do a free trial?" Two LIGO rounds have not resolved it — they are stuck on wanting to experience before committing.
Your objective: Use the experience-framing T.O.: "My goal is for you to leave today feeling completely confident. So here's what I'd love to do — let me set up a complimentary access to a studio class or a Thrive session so you can experience it. The best person to arrange that is [Manager's Name], who you met earlier. Let me grab them." Brief the manager on the trial request and the guest's X-Factor. Stay silent while the manager handles the trial framing and closes.
The experience T.O. is often the cleanest one because the framing is genuinely guest-centric. You are offering them something tangible. When the manager sets up the trial and then uses that positive momentum to close, the sequence feels natural and non-pressured.
Scenario 03 — The Early Introduction Practice Setup Drill
This is a practice drill for the early manager introduction — before any T.O. situation. You are in the greeting phase with a new guest. The manager is available and nearby.
Your objective: Find the natural, organic moment during the greeting or early pre-tour to introduce the manager without it feeling scripted or forced. The introduction should last 10–15 seconds maximum — warm, brief, and casual. "Hey [Manager] — meet [Guest's Name]. They're checking us out today." Manager responds warmly and briefly, then exits. Practice this until it feels like a genuine, spontaneous moment rather than a choreographed one.
The quality of every future T.O. you execute depends on how well you execute this early introduction. Practice it hundreds of times until it is completely natural. The goal: the guest should feel like they met a friendly person on the floor — not that they were introduced to the closing manager who will be brought in later.
Scenario 04 — Breaking the Silence (What Not to Do) Discipline Drill
The T.O. has been initiated. The manager is working with the guest. Midway through, the guest looks at you and says: "What do you think — is this a good deal?" The manager has not addressed you or asked you a question.
Your objective: Do not answer. Smile warmly, gesture slightly toward the manager, and say nothing — or at most: "I think [Manager's Name] has you covered." Then stop. Do not elaborate. Do not validate the manager's offer or add your opinion. Practice this moment specifically — it is the exact moment most consultants break the silence and undercut the T.O. The guest looking at you is not permission to speak. Only the manager asking you a direct question is.
This scenario exposes the hardest part of the T.O. — the silence when directly addressed. It feels rude not to answer. It is not. The manager is in control. Your answer, no matter how well-intentioned, dilutes their authority. Practice this moment until the redirect feels completely natural and not at all awkward.
