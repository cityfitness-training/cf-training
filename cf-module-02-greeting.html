The Objective

You Have 7 Seconds.

Research is consistent on this: people form lasting impressions within the first 7 seconds of meeting someone. In that window, they are not evaluating your knowledge of the membership tiers. They are evaluating whether they like and trust you. That decision drives everything that follows.

The Core Truth
A strong greeting does not just feel good — it does measurable sales work. A guest who trusts you from the first moment asks fewer defensive questions, reveals more during discovery, and says yes at a significantly higher rate. The greeting is not a pleasantry. It is the foundation of the close.

01 On Purpose — Your Non-Verbal Communication Before You Speak ▼

The moment a guest steps into your sightline, the greeting has already started. You have not spoken yet — but they are already reading you. Every non-verbal signal you send in those first moments is forming their first impression of you and of City Fitness.

There are four non-verbal elements that must be deliberate and on point every single time. Not most of the time. Every time.

👔
Appearance
Are you dressed to the City Fitness standard? Do you look like someone who belongs in a premium fitness environment? Your appearance communicates professionalism before you say a word.
🧍
Posture
Stand tall. Shoulders back and open. No slouching over the desk, no leaning on the counter. Open posture signals confidence and approachability simultaneously.
😊
Facial Expression
Genuine smile. Eyes engaged. Not a customer service performance smile — a real one. People detect fake smiles instantly. Your face should reflect that you are actually glad they came in.
🚶
Movement
Walk toward them with energy and purpose. Do not shuffle. Do not rush. Move like someone who is genuinely happy to greet them. The way you walk toward a guest tells them exactly how important they are.
Self-Check Habit
Before every shift, take 10 seconds at a mirror. Check all four: appearance, posture, expression, energy. Athletes warm up before they compete. You should too.
The Desk Trap
The single most common non-verbal failure: staying seated when a guest walks in. It communicates immediately that they are an interruption rather than the entire point of your day. Stand up. Every time. No exceptions.
02 Confidence — The Most Contagious Thing in the Room Internal State ▼

Confidence is not an act you put on for a guest. It is an internal state that leaks out whether you manage it or not. If you are uncertain about the value City Fitness offers, that uncertainty will show — in your voice, your posture, your eye contact, the pace of your words.

The guest is not just evaluating the club. They are evaluating whether you believe in it. And your belief — or lack of it — transfers directly to them. This is not motivational language. This is human psychology. People buy confidence as much as they buy products.

The Core Belief
Before every interaction, anchor yourself to this: "City Fitness has everything this person needs to achieve their goals. My job is to show them that." When that belief is genuine, your entire presence changes.

Here is what real member feedback sounds like — the kind that should fuel your confidence every single day:

Real Member — Fishtown
"I must say it is one of, if not the most admirable gym I've ever worked out in... I joined immediately and got my friend to join an hour later."
Real Member — Fishtown
"I originally joined this gym because it's close to my office, but I find myself coming here even on my days off — and I live 15 miles away. That says everything."

This is what you are selling. You are not selling a price point. You are selling an experience that people drive 15 miles out of their way for on their days off. Walk into every greeting knowing that.

"I am genuinely excited to help this person."
"City Fitness has everything they need."
"I am the right person to guide them."
03 Ask for Their Name First — Make It About Them The Opening Line ▼

Most people open with their own name. "Hi, I'm Mike, welcome to City Fitness!" It sounds friendly. It is also subtly self-centered. You have led with yourself.

The City Fitness standard flips this. You ask for their name first. One small change with a significant psychological effect: it signals immediately that this interaction is about them, not about you. They are the priority from the very first word.

The Standard Opening Line
"Hello, welcome to City Fitness! What's your name?"

What happens when you ask for their name first:

They feel prioritized. You have established before anything else that they are the focus of this conversation, not you.

Natural reciprocity. After giving their name, most people will naturally ask for yours. The exchange feels balanced and collaborative rather than transactional.

You now have their name. Use it. Not constantly — that becomes awkward — but strategically throughout the interaction. A person's name is the single most powerful word in any conversation.

Name Recall Tip
The moment they tell you their name, repeat it back immediately: "Great to meet you, [Name]!" This cements it in your memory and signals that you actually heard them — not just waiting for your turn to talk.
If You Miss It
If you did not catch their name clearly, ask again immediately and directly: "I'm sorry — say that one more time?" Do not guess. Do not skip it. Using the wrong name is worse than asking again.
04 Give Your Name — Complete the Exchange Reciprocity ▼

After they give you their name and you respond warmly, now is when you introduce yourself. The sequence matters: them first, you second. This is not a rule for its own sake — it is a psychological principle. When you establish their importance before your own, the reciprocity that follows feels genuine rather than scripted.

The Name Exchange
"Great to meet you, [Their Name]! I'm [Your Name] — I'm going to be taking care of you today."

The phrase "taking care of you today" is doing important work. It positions you as a host and guide — not a salesperson. It sets a tone of service and personal attention before the process has even started.

The Relationship Rule
People buy from people they like. And people like people who make them feel seen and valued. The name exchange — done in the right order, with genuine warmth — is the fastest way to start building that liking.
05 Lay Out Expectations — Frame the Process Setting the Stage ▼

After the name exchange, there is a moment of uncertainty for the guest. They do not know what is about to happen. That uncertainty — even if small — creates low-level anxiety. Your job is to remove it immediately by giving them a clear, simple picture of the next 45 minutes.

This is called laying out expectations. It is not a lengthy explanation. It is a three-part sentence that answers the question every guest has in the back of their mind: "What is this going to be like?"

The Framework Script
"So here's what we're going to do — first, we'll head back to my desk and chat a little about your fitness background. Then I'll take you on a quick lap so you can see everything we've got. And if you love it — which I think you will — I'll show you how memberships work. Sound fair?"

Why "Sound fair?" matters: it is a tie-down — a small agreement that gets the guest nodding and mentally aligned with the process before it begins. They have now implicitly committed to the structure. The tour feels collaborative rather than something being done to them.

The Psychology of Certainty
Anxiety thrives in uncertainty. The moment someone knows what to expect, their guard drops. A guest who is relaxed and certain about the process is significantly easier to build rapport with than one who is wondering when the sales pitch is coming.
Keep It Simple
Do not over-explain the process. Do not mention pricing in the framework. Do not make it sound like a formal appointment. It should feel like a friend showing them around. Three beats. One tie-down. Done.
06 "Come On Back" — Creating Instant Belonging Transition ▼

Three words that do more work than they look like they should. "Come on back."

On the surface, it is an invitation to follow you. Underneath, it is something more intentional. Even for a first-time visitor who has never been in the building, this phrase implies they already belong here. It is welcoming without being formal. It is casual without being dismissive. It sounds like something you say to someone who is already part of the community — not someone you are evaluating for membership.

The Transition
[After name exchange and framework] "Come on back." [Gesture warmly toward the desk or floor — walk alongside them, not ahead.]

The physical movement here matters. As you say "come on back," begin moving — and gesture for them to walk with you rather than behind you. Walking alongside a guest in these first moments communicates equality and partnership. Leading too far in front communicates that you are in charge and they are following, which subtly reinforces a power dynamic you do not want.

The Walk Standard
Alongside or slightly angled toward them — never more than one step ahead. This is both rapport-building and practical: it allows natural conversation, eye contact, and real-time body language reading from the first moment you move together.
07 The Complete Greeting — All 5 Elements in Flow Full Execution ▼

Every element we have covered works together as a single fluid sequence. When it is done right, it does not feel like a system — it feels like a genuine, warm human interaction. That is exactly the goal. Here is the full greeting executed from start to finish.

The Complete Greeting — Full Script
SCENARIOA guest walks through the front door. Consultant stands, smiles, and moves toward them with energy before they reach the desk.
Consultant"Hello! Welcome to City Fitness." [Genuine smile, direct eye contact, warm energy — already moving toward them.]
Consultant"What's your name?" [Asks before giving their own name — makes it about the guest first.]
Guest"I'm Sarah."
Consultant"Sarah, great to meet you! I'm [Name] — I'll be taking care of you today." [Handshake if natural. Genuine warmth. Uses her name immediately to cement it.]
Consultant"So here's what we're going to do — we'll head back to my desk and chat a little about your fitness background. Then I'll take you on a quick tour so you can see everything. And if you love it, I'll walk you through how memberships work. Sound fair?"
Guest"Yeah, sounds good."
Consultant"Perfect. Come on back, Sarah." [Gestures alongside — begins walking with them, not ahead.]

From door to desk, this entire exchange takes under 60 seconds. In that time, the guest has learned your name, felt prioritized, understood the process, and started walking through the club feeling like they already belong. That is the power of a great greeting done right.

The Practice Standard
This sequence should be practiced until it feels completely natural — not recited. Record yourself on your phone. Watch it back. Does it feel genuine or scripted? Keep going until you cannot tell the difference. That is when you have it.

✗ The Mistake

Stand every single time. No exceptions. Even if you are mid-task, put it down and stand up. This is non-negotiable.
✗ The Mistake

✓ The Fix
Always ask their name first. "What's your name?" comes before "I'm [Name]." Always. Without exception.
✗ The Mistake
Flat, formulaic opener: "Can I help you?" It sounds like a retail transaction, not a premium fitness experience.
✓ The Fix
"Hello, welcome to City Fitness! What's your name?" Warm, specific, and immediately focused on them.
✗ The Mistake
Skipping the framework. Guest has no idea what is about to happen. Uncertainty creates anxiety. Anxiety creates resistance.

Always frame the process in three beats: chat at desk, tour, memberships. Close with "Sound fair?" to get their first agreement.

Walking ahead of the guest during the transition — consultant leads, guest follows behind. Creates a follower dynamic, kills rapport.

Role-Play Scenarios
Practice

4 Scenarios to
Train On

The greeting needs to feel natural across every type of guest energy — from warm and excited to guarded and skeptical. Practice all four until the framework holds regardless of what walks through the door.

Scenario 01 — The Warm Walk-In Standard
Guest walks in with a smile, looks around with curiosity. They seem open and friendly. Clearly interested in joining.
Your objective: Execute the full greeting sequence cleanly — stand, move, name exchange in the right order, framework, "come on back." This is your baseline. Every element should land naturally and conversationally. Do not rush through the framework just because they seem easy. This is where habits are built.
The warm walk-in is a trap for complacency. Because they are already friendly, it is tempting to skip steps and jump straight to rapport building. Do not. Every element of the greeting exists for a reason. Execute all of it.
Scenario 02 — The Skeptical Guest Defensive Energy
Guest walks in with guarded body language — arms slightly crossed, minimal eye contact, flat affect. They have clearly been in gyms before and are ready for a sales pitch they do not want.
Your objective: Do not mirror their energy or shrink from it. Match their pace — calm, unhurried, genuine — but do not reduce your warmth. The framework is especially important here: telling a guarded guest exactly what to expect immediately removes their biggest anxiety (that they are walking into a high-pressure situation). "Sound fair?" should land like a relief.
Guarded guests become your biggest advocates when they feel genuinely respected and not sold to. The greeting is your single best tool for disarming that defense. Confidence and genuine warmth are the only things that work. Trying harder or louder makes it worse.
Scenario 03 — The Rushed Guest Time Pressure
Guest opens with: "I only have about 20 minutes — I just wanted to take a quick look around." They are already signaling that they want control of the timeline.
Your objective: Acknowledge their timeline without surrendering the process. Do not skip the framework — compress it. "Totally — we can absolutely do a quick version of this. Give me 15 minutes and I'll show you everything worth seeing. What's your name?" The greeting still happens. The framework still gets set. You just calibrate the promise to their constraint.
The time objection at the greeting stage is almost always a test, not a real limitation. Most "I only have 20 minutes" guests end up staying for the full tour if you are engaging and efficient. Respect the timeline they gave you — but do not let it collapse the process before it starts.
Scenario 04 — The Couple or Group Multiple Guests
Two people walk in together — a couple, or two friends checking out the club at the same time. There is no clear "lead" guest.
Your objective: Get both names. Not just the one who speaks first. Make eye contact with both. Address both in the framework. The risk here is that you naturally gravitate toward the more talkative or forward person and the other person feels invisible — which means you have lost half the room before the tour begins. Equal warmth, both names, both acknowledged.
In couple or group tours, the person who is quieter often has more decision-making weight than the one who talks. Do not underestimate or under-engage the quiet one. Getting their name and directing occasional eye contact and questions toward them is essential.
