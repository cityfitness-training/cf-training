The Objective

Show Them Their Life Here.

The purpose of the tour is not to show them the equipment. It is to help them visualize themselves as a City Fitness member — already living the outcome they described in the pre-tour. Every feature you show should answer one unspoken question: how does this help me specifically?

The Core Standard
A great tour is a personalized story — not a property tour. The guest should leave thinking "they built this place for me," not "that was a nice gym." That distinction is the difference between a close and a walkaway.

01 Feature → Advantage → Benefit — The F.A.B. Framework Foundation ▼

Every stop on the tour should follow the same three-step framework. Feature alone tells them what something is. Advantage tells them what it does. Benefit tells them what it means for their life specifically. Most consultants stop at Feature. The close lives in Benefit.

F
Feature
What it is. The factual description of the amenity or equipment.
"This is our Infrared Sauna."
A
Advantage
What it does. The general benefit of this feature for anyone.
"It penetrates deep into muscle tissue to accelerate recovery and reduce inflammation."
B
Benefit
What it means for THIS person based on what they told you.
"You mentioned your back has been giving you trouble — members use this specifically for that. It's like having a recovery spa available every single day."
The Standard
Never present a Feature without landing on the Benefit. And the Benefit must always connect back to something they told you in the pre-tour. If you cannot connect a feature to their goals, keep moving and come back to it later — or skip it.

The F.A.B. framework is your tour engine. Every stop. Every amenity. Feature → Advantage → Benefit, personalized to their X-Factor. Practice it until it feels like natural conversation, not a script.

02 Walk Alongside — The Positioning Rule Body Language ▼

Where you walk in relation to the guest tells them everything about the dynamic you are creating. Walk too far ahead and you are a tour guide — they are a tourist following someone around a place that is not theirs. Walk alongside and you are an equal, a partner, a collaborator showing them something you are genuinely excited about.

The Rule
Never more than one step ahead. Alongside or slightly angled toward them at every stop. This is non-negotiable throughout the entire tour.

Walking alongside also has practical benefits that directly serve your sales process:

It enables real conversation. When you are side by side, conversation flows naturally. When you are leading from the front, they are talking to the back of your head.

It lets you read body language. When you are next to them, you can see when their eyes light up at a piece of equipment or when they glance away with disinterest. These signals are your real-time feedback — they tell you when to slow down and when to keep moving.

It creates physical parity. People trust people who treat them as equals. Walking ahead is a power move — even subconsciously. Walking alongside says "we are in this together."

At Each Stop
Turn toward them when you speak — not toward the equipment. You are talking to a person, not narrating a display case. Eye contact during F.A.B. delivery seals the connection between the feature and their personal benefit.
03 Connect Every Stop to Their X-Factor Personalization ▼

Your notes from the pre-tour are your tour script. Everything the guest told you — their X-Factor, their goals, their past struggles, their emotional language — is the raw material you are going to use to make every stop feel personally relevant. This is what separates a great tour from a good one.

At each major stop, ask yourself one question before you speak: "How does this connect to what they told me?" If you can answer that, you have your transition. If you cannot, either skip the stop or save it for overall value.

X-Factor Callbacks — Examples in Action
SETUPGuest said in pre-tour: "I want to feel like a better dad. I get winded playing with my kids."
At Cardio"You mentioned getting winded with your kids — this is exactly where that changes. Our members who work on Zone 2 cardio here — three or four times a week — see noticeable improvements in their day-to-day energy within the first month."
At Revive"You mentioned you have a demanding schedule — this area is what makes consistency sustainable. Recovery is the thing most people skip, and it's why they burn out. Having this available daily means you come back fresher every time."
At Studio"These classes run early morning and evening — so your schedule isn't an excuse anymore. And a lot of dads come to these with their partners, which makes the accountability part automatic."
Use Their Exact Words
If they said "I want to feel like myself again" — use that phrase back to them: "This is the area where our members tell us they start feeling like themselves again." Hearing their own words reflected back creates an immediate emotional connection that generic language never achieves.
04 Show the Entire Club — Never Assume, Always Show Full Value ▼

One of the most common tour mistakes is editing the experience based on what the guest said they are interested in. They mentioned cardio — so you skip the WE/FIT floor. They said they are not a class person — so you walk past the studio. This is a costly error.

People do not always know what they want until they see it. The guest who said they "just want to use the treadmill" might stop dead in their tracks when they see the WE/FIT space lit up and full of energy. That moment of unexpected excitement is something you cannot manufacture — but you can create the conditions for it by showing everything.

The Rule
Show the entire club on every tour. Every area. No exceptions based on assumed interest. Frame everything as part of the access they get — even if they never use it, it contributes to the perceived value of the membership.
Framing an Area They Did Not Mention
"Even if this isn't something you'd use right away, it's part of everything you have access to — and a lot of members surprise themselves once they try it. We have members who came in saying they'd never take a group class and now it's the main reason they come."

The studio and WE/FIT spaces in particular are high-visual-impact areas. The energy, the lighting, the setup — they create an emotional response even in someone who is skeptical about classes. Always walk through. Let the space do the selling.

05 Tie-Down Questions — Building the "Yes" Chain Micro-Closes ▼

A tie-down question is a subtle agreement-gathering technique used throughout the tour. Its purpose is to accumulate a chain of small "yes" answers that makes the final "yes" at the close feel like a natural continuation — not a sudden leap.

Every time a guest says "yes" to a tie-down question during the tour, they are mentally moving closer to membership. By the time you sit down to present pricing, you have a guest who has already agreed with you a dozen times. Saying no becomes the inconsistency — not saying yes.

Tie-Down Question Bank
"Wouldn't you agree this could help you target those goals directly?" — "Could you see yourself using this a couple times a week?" — "Does this look like the kind of environment you'd feel comfortable training in?" — "If you had access to this every day, would you take advantage of it?" — "Can you see how this connects to what you're trying to accomplish?"

Notice what all of these have in common: they are all questions the guest can only honestly answer "yes" to if you have done a good job connecting the feature to their goals. A tie-down question that the guest struggles to answer yes to is a signal — you did not connect the feature to their benefit effectively enough. Go back and rebuild the F.A.B.

Do Not Overuse
Two to three tie-down questions per tour stop maximum. If every sentence ends with a question, it starts to feel like a pattern and the guest becomes aware of the technique. Use them at moments of genuine enthusiasm or impact — not mechanically.
06 Offer Demonstrations — Eliminate Intimidation Engagement ▼

When a guest slows down near a piece of equipment, asks about it, or hesitates — that hesitation is almost always one of two things: curiosity or intimidation. Both are invitations for the same response: get them involved.

A brief, confident demonstration transforms a piece of equipment from something foreign and potentially scary into something familiar and accessible. The moment a guest's hands are on something — or they see it working — their relationship with it changes.

The Demo Invitation
"Want me to show you how this works real quick? It's one of those things that looks more complicated than it is." [Demonstrate — 30 seconds max. Then:] "See? Super intuitive. And if you ever want a refresher, every machine has a QR code right here — scan it and it pulls up a full instructional video. So you're never left guessing."

The QR code detail is important. It tells the guest two things: the club has invested in helping them learn, and they do not have to feel embarrassed about asking for help because the help is already built in. For Never Fitness guests especially, this removes a major anxiety about looking lost or out of place.

The Hands-On Rule
If a guest touches or tries something during the tour, their likelihood of joining increases significantly. Get them involved wherever possible — adjust a seat, try a grip, scan a QR code. Physical interaction builds ownership. Ownership drives commitment.
07 Showcase Cleanliness — A Core Value in Action Differentiation ▼

Cleanliness is not a nice-to-have at City Fitness. It is a Core Value — one of the three pillars we stand on alongside Smile and Cutting Edge. That means it should be actively showcased during the tour, not just assumed or hoped that the guest notices.

Most people have had a bad gym experience. Dirty equipment, sticky floors, locker rooms that smell like a middle school. City Fitness is the antidote to that experience — but they will not feel it unless you point it out with intention and pride.

Cleanliness Callout Script
"One thing I always want to make sure people notice — and this is something we take really seriously here — is how clean this facility is. We have a dedicated Clean Team working throughout every operating hour. Not just opening and closing. All day. It's one of our three core values and you'll feel the difference."

If a member of the Clean Team is nearby and is approachable, introduce them. This is a powerful moment. It demonstrates that City Fitness values every role on the team — and it shows genuine pride in the facility that most gyms cannot replicate.

Why This Matters to the Close
Cleanliness addresses an unspoken objection almost every guest carries from past gym experiences. By proactively calling it out, you eliminate a reason to leave — before they even think to bring it up. It is objection prevention built into the tour.
08 Plant the Referral Seed — The Buddy System Moment Referral Setup ▼

The referral ask at the end of the sale is far more effective when the seed has been planted during the tour. The tour gives you a natural, organic moment to introduce the idea of bringing someone — and it frames it as a member benefit rather than a sales tactic.

The moment: when you pass two members working out together. This is your cue.

The Referral Seed Script
"See those two over there? This is actually something we see a lot here — members who come with a friend or partner. Research shows that workout partners increase consistency by 30%, and honestly you can just see it — they push each other harder and they're having more fun doing it. We have a really generous guest pass policy, so when you join, bringing your people is easy. We'll revisit that at the end."

That last line — "we'll revisit that at the end" — is important. It sets up the referral ask as something they already agreed to hear more about. When you get to it after the close, it does not feel like a new request — it is a follow-through on something you mentioned on the tour.

The Four Reasons Partners Matter
1. Members with workout buddies train more consistently.   2. They stay committed longer.   3. They train harder with someone to push them.   4. They enjoy it more and that enjoyment keeps them coming back.

Common Mistakes
What Kills the Tour

6 Tour Mistakes
& How to Fix Them

✗ The Mistake
The museum tour — walking through and naming things: "This is the cardio area. This is the weight room. This is the sauna." No benefit. No personalization. No connection.
✓ The Fix
Every stop needs a Benefit that connects to their pre-tour. Feature → Advantage → Benefit. If you cannot name the Benefit for this specific person, you are not ready to present that stop.
✗ The Mistake
Walking ahead of the guest. You are giving a property tour, not a shared exploration. They feel like they are being led, not included.
✓ The Fix
Alongside. Always. If you catch yourself more than one step ahead, slow down. Turn toward them. Re-engage. The physical positioning is the rapport.
✗ The Mistake
Skipping areas because they "won't be interested." You are making an assumption that costs you perceived value and potential excitement you cannot get back.
✓ The Fix
Show everything. Frame every area as part of their access. "Even if you never use this, it's yours." Unexpected excitement at any stop can change the entire energy of the close.
✗ The Mistake
No tie-downs. The guest nods politely through the whole tour and you have no idea where they stand. You get to the price card with zero momentum built.
✓ The Fix
2–3 tie-down questions per major stop. "Could you see yourself using this?" "Wouldn't you agree this addresses what you mentioned?" Small agreements compound. By the desk, they have already said yes a dozen times.
✗ The Mistake
Talking about equipment without connecting it to their goals. "This is a Concept2 rower — great machine." Great. So what? Why does it matter to them?
✓ The Fix
Every feature needs a "for you" statement. "This is a Concept2 rower — it's full body, zero impact on the joints, which given what you mentioned about your knee is actually an ideal starting point for cardio."
✗ The Mistake
Forgetting to plant the referral seed. The referral ask at the end lands cold if no groundwork was laid during the tour.
✓ The Fix
Use the buddy moment on the floor. Point it out naturally when you pass members together. Set up the "we'll revisit this at the end" so the referral ask feels like a follow-through, not a surprise.
Role-Play Scenarios
Practice

4 Tour Scenarios
to Train On

Scenario 01 — The Avid Exerciser Tour Bucket 1
Guest is a seasoned athlete — trains six days a week, knows their way around a gym, has specific performance goals. They are evaluating City Fitness against other premium options they have toured.
Your objective: Match their expertise level — do not over-explain basics. Focus on advanced equipment, cutting-edge features (Revive, specific strength tools), and differentiation they will not find elsewhere. Ask about plateaus: "What's the one thing you've been trying to crack that your current setup hasn't solved?" Make them feel like they are talking to a peer, not a salesperson. Skip introductory framing and spend more time in the areas that genuinely excite them.
The Avid Exerciser will test your knowledge. If they ask about something you do not know — say so honestly: "That's outside my expertise but we have a Thrive coach I'd love to introduce you to who lives in that world." Authenticity over false confidence every time.
Scenario 02 — The Never Fitness Tour Bucket 3
Guest has never had a gym membership. They are visibly nervous, using phrases like "I don't really know where to start" and "I don't want to look like I don't know what I'm doing." Every unfamiliar machine creates a small wave of anxiety.
Your objective: Slow down. Simplify your language — no jargon. At every machine that creates hesitation, offer a quick demo and show the QR code. Introduce staff members warmly. Emphasize that nobody knows how to do everything on day one and that City Fitness is set up specifically to support people at the beginning. Focus the tour on the most approachable areas first and build confidence before moving to more complex spaces.
The Never Fitness guest is deciding whether they belong here — not whether the equipment is good. Your job is to make them feel like they absolutely do. Every introduction, every demo, every laugh you share on the tour is answering that question. Build belonging before you build the case for membership.
Scenario 03 — The Distracted Guest Engagement Challenge
Halfway through the tour the guest starts checking their phone, giving one-word responses, and drifting ahead of you to look at things independently. Their energy has dropped and you are losing them.
Your objective: Re-engage without desperation. Stop and ask directly: "Is there a specific area you want to spend more time in? I want to make sure we're covering what matters most to you." Let them lead for a moment — follow their curiosity. Re-anchor to their X-Factor: "Before we keep going — you mentioned wanting to feel like yourself again. Let me make sure I show you the one thing I think is going to resonate most with that." Regain the thread by returning to what they care about.
A distracted guest is usually a guest whose curiosity has not been activated yet. Go back to their X-Factor — that is the thing that lights people up. The phone goes away when something in front of them becomes more interesting than what is on the screen.
Scenario 04 — The Question Firehose High-Engagement Guest
The guest asks a question at every single stop. Class schedules, equipment brands, staff certifications, guest policy details, parking — they want to know everything. The tour is running long and you have not gotten to Revive yet.
Your objective: Answer enthusiastically — this guest's questions are buying signals, not obstacles. But manage the pace: "Great question — let me answer that and then there's something else I really want to show you before we circle back to the desk." Triage: answer questions that connect to their goals fully, park others for the desk: "I'll have all the class schedule details at the desk and I can walk you through everything there." Do not rush them — channel their energy, do not fight it.
The question firehose guest is the highest-intent guest in the building. Every question is an expression of interest. The consultant who gets frustrated by too many questions is leaving a sale on the table. Lean in, stay warm, keep moving.
