01 Why POS Timing Is Everything The Window ▼

The single most important principle of the referral play is timing. And there is only one right moment: immediately after they join, while they are still at the desk, while the enthusiasm and relief of the decision is alive in the room.

Here is what happens emotionally the moment someone makes a significant commitment: they feel a rush of excitement — and simultaneously, a flicker of doubt. Did I make the right call? Is this worth it? This is completely normal human psychology. What resolves that doubt fastest is sharing the decision — having someone validate it, ideally by joining too.

The Psychology of POS Referrals
New members refer at POS primarily for three reasons: they want to validate their own decision, they genuinely want to share something they are excited about, and the perks for themselves and their friends make the ask feel mutual. All three motivations are at their peak in the first five minutes after signing. Every minute that passes, they fade.

This is why asking for referrals 30 days later dramatically underperforms POS. After 30 days, the initial enthusiasm has become routine. The decision no longer needs validation. The emotional energy that powers a referral ask has been replaced by habit. Strike at POS — every time, without exception.

The Referral Stat
Members who work out with a partner are 30% more consistent, stay committed longer, train harder, and enjoy the experience more. This is not a sales number — it is a genuine member success data point. Lead with it. It reframes the referral ask from "do me a favor" to "let me help you succeed."
02 The "Why" Before the "What" — Explain Benefits First The Setup ▼

The single most common referral ask mistake is leading with the ask: "Do you know anyone who might want to join?" This is transactional, feels sudden, and puts the new member on the spot before they understand why they should say yes.

The correct sequence is always: explain why referrals matter first, then explain the perks for both parties, then address trust, then ask for names. By the time you ask, they have already agreed mentally — because they understand the value on both sides.

The Why Before the What — Setup Script
"Now it's time for the most important thing we do for our new members. Research consistently shows that people who work out with a friend or partner are 30% more consistent, stay committed significantly longer, and honestly just have a better time. We want you to succeed here — and having someone with you is one of the best things you can do for your own results. So we have a program specifically designed to make that easy for you."

Notice what this does: it frames the referral program as a member success tool — not a sales tool. You are not asking them to help City Fitness grow. You are telling them that inviting a friend is one of the most effective things they can do for themselves. That reframe changes everything about how the ask lands.

03 The Perks — What's In It for Everyone The Benefits ▼

The referral program works because it is genuinely a win-win-win. The new member wins. Their friends win. City Fitness wins. Understanding exactly what each party gets — and being able to communicate it clearly — is what makes the ask feel generous rather than transactional.

👤
For the Member
50 Points

🎫
For Their Friend
7-Day VIP Access
A full week of complete club access — a $75 value — exclusively available through member referral. Not available any other way.
🤝
For Both
A Workout Buddy
The accountability that drives 30% better consistency. The motivation that keeps them both coming back. The enjoyment that makes it sustainable.
Key Point — The 7-Day Pass Is Exclusive
Emphasize that the 7-Day VIP pass is only available through member referral. It cannot be purchased. It cannot be found online. Their friends can only access it through them. This exclusivity makes the invite feel like a genuine gift — not a gym promotion.
Three Member Motivations
1. What's in it for them (points, buddy, validation).   2. What's in it for their friend (free week, exclusive access).   3. Can I trust you with my contacts (the 24-hour promise). Address all three — in that order.
04 The 24-Hour Promise — Addressing the Trust Concern Building Trust ▼

The most common unspoken hesitation in the referral ask is not about the perks — it is about trust. The new member is thinking: "If I give you my friends' numbers, are they going to get called and feel like I put them on a spam list?" That concern, if not addressed proactively, is what makes people hand over one name instead of ten.

The 24-Hour Promise is how you eliminate that concern before it forms.

The 24-Hour Promise Script
"And here's our promise to you: I won't contact anyone on this list for 24 hours. If you change your mind about anyone you've written down — just text or call me, and I will remove them before I reach out. You have complete control. Sound fair?"

This one short commitment does something powerful: it shifts the dynamic from "giving up control of your contacts" to "staying in control of who gets contacted." The new member is no longer handing you names — they are choosing to share an opportunity with people they care about, with the safety net of being able to pull anyone back within 24 hours.

Why the 24-Hour Window Works
In practice, almost no one calls to remove a name. The 24-hour promise reduces the anxiety of providing the name in the first place — which means they provide more names. More names means more contacts. More contacts means more conversions. The promise costs you nothing and earns you trust worth everything.
05 The Procedure — How to Execute the Ask The System ▼

The referral ask has a specific physical procedure that consistently produces better results than a casual, improvised ask. Every element of the procedure is intentional.

1
The Transition Line
Use a specific phrase to signal importance and create anticipation. Do not transition casually.
"Now it's time for the most important thing we do for our new members."
2
Sit Down With Them
Physically sit down alongside the new member — not across from them. This creates a collaborative, side-by-side energy rather than a desk transaction. It communicates that this is something you are doing together, not to them.
3
Present a Full Sheet of Paper
Not a small card. A full sheet. This communicates that you are expecting real names — not one or two as an afterthought. The visual size of the sheet sets an expectation of volume.
"Just write down anyone who comes to mind — friends, family, coworkers. Don't overthink it."
4
Aim for 10 Names
Ten is the target. Most members will give fewer — but anchoring high produces more names than anchoring low. "Who are the 10 people you'd want as your accountability group?" is a fundamentally different question than "Do you know anyone?"
5
Guide the Brainstorm
If they stall after two or three names, prompt by category: "Anyone from work? Any neighbors you see regularly? Family members you've been meaning to motivate?" Categorized prompts unlock people they would not have thought of otherwise.
6
Deliver the 24-Hour Promise
After they have written names, confirm the promise: "I won't contact anyone for 24 hours — if anyone on this list changes your mind, just let me know and I'll remove them."

07 The Four Reasons Buddies Matter — Know Them Cold The Case ▼

Before you ask for referrals, you need to be able to articulate — genuinely and specifically — why having a workout partner is one of the most powerful things a new member can do for their success. These four reasons are not sales language. They are real member outcomes backed by consistent data. Know them well enough to say them naturally, not recite them.

1
Consistency. Members who have a workout partner are 30% more consistent in attendance than those who work out alone. The accountability of another person is the most powerful consistency driver available — more than apps, more than schedules, more than willpower.
2
Commitment longevity. Members who train with a friend stay members significantly longer. When the motivation to go for yourself wavers — and it always does at some point — the commitment to not let your partner down carries you through.
3
Performance. People train harder when someone is watching. Not aggressively harder — encouragingly harder. A friend who celebrates your progress and pushes you through the last set is a performance enhancer that no equipment can replicate.
4
Enjoyment. Working out with someone you like is simply more fun. And things that are fun get done. The members who stick longest are the ones who genuinely look forward to coming in — and a social element is one of the biggest drivers of that enjoyment.
Connect to Their X-Factor
When you deliver the buddy reasons, personalize them. If their X-Factor was consistency ("I always start and stop"), lead with reason #1. If they mentioned enjoyment or dreading working out alone, lead with reason #4. The right reason for the right person hits differently than all four delivered equally.

4 Referral Scenarios
to Train On

Scenario 01 — The Enthusiastic New Member High Energy
Member just signed, is visibly excited, and says: "I can't wait to get started — this place is amazing!" The energy in the room is perfect.
Your objective: Use their enthusiasm as the bridge into the referral ask. "That's exactly the energy I love hearing — and that's exactly why I want to talk about one more thing before you head out." Execute the full referral sequence: transition line, sit alongside, why before what, buddy reasons, VIP perk explanation, 24-hour promise, full sheet, aim for 10 names. This scenario is about riding the energy wave — not letting it peak and then awkwardly pivoting to a new ask. The transition should feel like a natural continuation of the celebration.
High-energy new members produce the most referrals when the ask is positioned as an extension of their excitement — "You love it here? Let's get your people in here with you." Make the invite feel like sharing something great, not fulfilling a sales obligation.
Scenario 02 — "I Don't Really Know Anyone Who'd Be Interested" Hesitation
After the referral setup, the member says: "I appreciate it, but I don't really think any of my friends would be interested in a gym."
Your objective: Do not accept this and move on. Gently challenge the assumption: "I totally get that feeling — but you'd be surprised. Most people are more open to it than they let on, especially when it's a week for free with no pressure. Who in your life has mentioned wanting to get healthier, or who used to be active and kind of fell off? It doesn't have to be someone who is already a gym person." Then prompt by category: work, family, old friends. Aim to get at least 3–5 names even from a reluctant member.
The "nobody would be interested" response almost always means the member has not been asked to think categorically. They picture their most gym-unlikely friend and project that onto everyone. Category prompts break this by directing them to specific, different social networks where active or health-curious people live.
Scenario 03 — "I'll Think About Who to Recommend Later" Timing Objection
The member is warm and cooperative but says: "Can I think about it and send you some names later? I don't want to give you anyone right now without asking them first."
Your objective: Acknowledge their consideration for their friends, then make the case for right now: "I love that you're thoughtful about it — that's exactly the kind of person your friends are lucky to have. Here's the thing though: the VIP pass is something I can only give out at this moment as part of your new member setup. And honestly, the names you write down right now are the ones that are top of mind — those are the people you're most excited about sharing this with. Let's just put them down — you can always ask them yourself later, or I can reach out on your behalf with the offer. Either way works." Aim to get at least a partial list before they leave.
The "I'll send names later" response converts at dramatically lower rates than POS. Once they leave, the enthusiasm fades, the follow-through drops, and the list rarely materializes. The goal is not to pressure them — it is to capture the moment before the window closes. A partial list today beats a promised full list that never arrives.
Scenario 04 — Stalling at 2–3 Names Brainstorm Coaching
The member writes down two names quickly, then stares at the sheet and says: "I can't really think of anyone else right now."
Your objective: Guide the brainstorm with category prompts — gently and without pressure. "That's a great start. Let me help you think through a few categories. Anyone at work who has mentioned wanting to be more active? A neighbor you see regularly? Any family members — siblings, cousins — who have talked about wanting to make a change? Old friends from school or a previous job who used to work out?" Each prompt should be delivered as a genuine brainstorm helper, not a pressure tactic. Pause after each one and let them think. The goal is 7–10 — but even 5 quality names is a strong result.
Category prompting works because people's social networks are organized by context — work people, neighborhood people, family, old friends. When you ask generically, they access their most active social circle only. When you name specific contexts, they access different networks and surface names that would never have come up otherwise.
